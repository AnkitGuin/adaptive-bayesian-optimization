# Adaptive Bayesian Optimization under Non-Stationary and Noisy Objectives

**A study of exploration–exploitation strategies for sequential optimization
under changing conditions**

## Abstract

Bayesian optimization (BO) is a standard tool for expensive black-box
objectives, but its standard formulation assumes a stationary objective.
When the objective changes over time, an optimizer that keeps trusting
stale data keeps querying regions that no longer contain the optimum. We
study this on controlled synthetic benchmarks (1D/2D moving peaks, a
drifting sinusoid, a three-peak landscape with amplitude hand-over) plus a
3D process-efficiency simulator, under five non-stationarity regimes —
stationary, slow drift, abrupt change, random-walk optimum, and
observation-noise shift — at four noise levels and 20 independent seeds,
comparing random search, static GP+{EI, UCB, PI}, pure exploitation,
time-decayed BO (ABO), and our drift-aware strategy (DA-BO). DA-BO converts
time-decay weights into heteroscedastic GP observation noise (an exact
posterior), monitors standardized GP residuals with a Page–Hinkley test,
and responds to detected drift with a temporary exploration boost and
shortened surrogate memory. Cumulative regret, computed from noise-free
values, shows that DA-BO reduces regret by **~76% under abrupt 1D change
(67.1 → 16.4)**, **~64% under slow 1D drift (74.2 → 26.7)**, and **~61%
under peak-amplitude hand-over (63.7 → 24.8)**, all with paired-bootstrap
CIs excluding zero. Ablations attribute the gains primarily to drift
detection (removing it costs +243% regret) and the post-drift exploration
boost (+100%). DA-BO is *not* uniformly better: it pays a significant
stationary cost (+26–110% depending on noise), is hurt by pure
noise-level shifts (+56%), and — notably — does not help on the 2D moving
peak (slow drift +17%; abrupt not significantly different), a failure mode
we analyze explicitly. In the 3D application, where the optimum moves
slowly relative to the observation noise, DA-BO produces no drift
detections and reduces to ABO; static UCB and EI perform best there.

## 1. Introduction

Optimizing an objective that is expensive to evaluate is a recurring
problem: a single evaluation may be a physical experiment, a long
simulation, or a costly model test. **Bayesian optimization** addresses
this by fitting a probabilistic surrogate — most commonly a Gaussian
process (GP) — to past evaluations and using an **acquisition function**
(expected improvement, upper confidence bound, probability of improvement)
to choose the next evaluation point. The surrogate's uncertainty estimate
is what makes BO sample-efficient: it formalizes the classic
**exploration–exploitation trade-off**.

Standard BO, however, assumes the objective is **stationary**: the same
function is evaluated at every iteration. This assumption breaks in many
practical settings — environmental changes or seasonality shift the
response surface; machine degradation drifts the operating optimum;
changing demand changes which configuration is best. When the objective
changes, an optimizer that keeps trusting stale data will keep querying
regions that no longer contain the optimum. The research question of this
project is therefore:

> **How should Bayesian optimization adapt its exploration–exploitation
> strategy when the objective is noisy and changes over time?**

We frame the problem as **tracking a moving optimum**:

    x*_t = argmax_x f_t(x),   R_t = f_t(x*_t) − f_t(x_t),

and determine empirically *when* adaptation helps, *how much* noise it can
tolerate, and *when* it hurts.

### Contributions

1. A compact, fully reproducible implementation of GP-based BO in which the
   core GP, acquisition functions, acquisition optimizer, and sequential
   loop are our own code (scikit-learn is used only to cross-validate the
   GP implementation; posterior means agree to ~1e-7).
2. An **adaptive GP** in which time-decay weights are converted into
   heteroscedastic observation noise, giving an *exact* GP posterior that
   down-weights stale observations (no pseudo-observations, no
   approximation).
3. **DA-BO**, a drift-aware adaptive optimizer combining time-decay
   forgetting, Page–Hinkley drift detection on standardized residuals, and
   a recovery window with exploration boost and shortened memory.
4. A controlled experimental study (five non-stationarity regimes, four
   noise levels, dimensions 1–3, 20 seeds, 7 methods) with
   regret/tracking/recovery metrics, ablations, sensitivity analysis, and
   one real-world-inspired 3D application.
5. An honest failure analysis: DA-BO is *not* uniformly better — we report
   exactly where and why (Sections 6.6 and 9).

Per the project framing, we do **not** claim a fundamentally new BO
algorithm; the contribution is the development and *empirical evaluation*
of an adaptive acquisition strategy for changing objectives.

## 2. Research questions and hypothesis

**Primary hypothesis (H1).** *A drift-aware adaptive BO strategy achieves
lower cumulative regret and tracks a moving optimum more effectively than
standard stationary BO under non-stationary objectives, while avoiding
excessive exploration.*

**Verdict (from §6):** H1 is **confirmed for 1D objectives under abrupt
change, slow drift, and amplitude hand-over**, and **rejected for 2D
moving peaks, pure noise shifts, and stationary objectives**. The
hypothesis was not assumed; the experiments delineate its boundary.

| # | Question | Answer (short) |
|---|----------|----------------|
| RQ1 | How well does standard BO work on stationary objectives? | GP+UCB/exploration beat random search ~10×; EI ≈ PI; UCB best (Table 2). |
| RQ2 | How does observation noise affect acquisition performance? | PI degrades fastest under high noise (f_best contamination); UCB is most robust (Tables 2, 6). |
| RQ3 | What happens when the objective changes? | Static UCB collapses on abrupt 1D (CR 67.1, never recovers); EI is surprisingly resilient (20.1). |
| RQ4 | Does time-decayed weighting help on a changing objective? | Partially: ABO alone rescues a little (abrupt 1D 62.8 vs 67.1) but cannot escape a confidently wrong GP. |
| RQ5 | Does an adaptive acquisition choose better explore/exploit trade-offs? | Yes, when the detector fires at the right time (abrupt 1D, switch); no, in the 2D slow-drift case. |
| RQ6 | How quickly should old observations become irrelevant? | Half-life 16–32 iterations is optimal for a 100-evaluation budget (Fig. 8); ∞ is worst under drift. |
| RQ7 | Robustness across abrupt/gradual/high-noise/moving/amplitude regimes? | Robust in 1D; gains shrink with dimension and with noise; no gain in the 3D application. |
| RQ8 | When does adaptive BO perform worse? | Stationary (+26–110%), pure noise shift (+56%), slow 2D drift (+17%), slow random walk (+20%). |

## 3. Background

### 3.1 Gaussian processes

A GP prior `f ~ GP(0, k(·,·))` is fully specified by a kernel `k`. With
observations `y = f(X) + ε`, `ε ~ N(0, σ² I)`, the posterior at new points
`X*` is Gaussian:

    μ(x*)   = k*ᵀ (K + σ² I)⁻¹ y
    σ²(x*)  = k(x*,x*) − k*ᵀ (K + σ² I)⁻¹ k*

We implement these equations directly in numpy with a deterministic
Cholesky solver (increasing-jitter fallback), target standardisation, and
isotropic Matérn 5/2 (default), Matérn 3/2, and RBF kernels:

    k₅/₂(r) = (1 + √5 r + 5r²/3) e^{−√5 r},   r = ‖x−x′‖/ℓ

The implementation is cross-validated in the test suite against
scikit-learn's `GaussianProcessRegressor` on identical data (posterior mean
agrees to ~1.7e-7; variances agree up to sklearn's convention of including
the WhiteKernel noise at the test point).

### 3.2 Acquisition functions (maximization)

* **EI**: `EI(x) = (μ−f_best−ξ)Φ(Z) + σφ(Z)`, `Z = (μ−f_best−ξ)/σ`
* **UCB**: `UCB(x) = μ(x) + β σ(x)`
* **PI**: `PI(x) = Φ(Z)`

All are implemented with guarded zero-variance limits (no NaNs). The
acquisition function is maximised deterministically: dense grid + adaptive
local refinement (two batched refinement rounds per refined cell), which
resolves the acquisition maximum to ~10⁻⁵ of the domain width in 1D and
~2×10⁻³ in 2D while being fully vectorised.

### 3.3 Regret and tracking

With a *known* noise-free optimum `x*_t`, we define

    instantaneous regret  R_t = f_t(x*_t) − f_t(x_t)
    cumulative regret     CR_T = Σ_{t≤T} R_t
    tracking error        ‖x_t − x*_t‖ (distance to the nearest equivalent optimum)
    recovery time         first t ≥ t_change with f_t(x_t) ≥ f_t(x*_t) − 0.05 f_t(x*_t)
    adaptation regret     Σ of R_t over the 30-iteration post-change window

**Critically, all metrics use noise-free values**; the noisy observation is
never allowed to contaminate the metrics. The optimizer itself only ever
sees `y_t`.

### 3.4 Non-stationary optimization

Under a changing objective, past observations become stale. Two
complementary ideas are used here:

* **Forgetting**: weight observations by recency. We convert weights into
  per-observation noise (Section 4.2), which is exact for the GP posterior.
* **Change detection**: monitor how surprised the surrogate is by new
  observations (standardized residuals) and react when the surprise level
  shifts (Page–Hinkley, Section 4.3).

## 4. Methodology

### 4.1 Static BO (baseline)

    1. collect n₀ initial observations (Latin hypercube)
    2. fit GP to all history
    3. maximise acquisition → x_t
    4. evaluate y_t = f_t(x_t) + ε_t
    5. append observation, refit, repeat until budget T

All historical observations remain equally relevant. Implemented for EI,
UCB, PI, and pure exploitation (`a(x) = μ(x)`).

### 4.2 Time-decayed (weighted) GP — "ABO"

At iteration `t`, observation `i` (made at `t_i`) has age `a_i = t − t_i`
and weight

    w_i = exp(−λ a_i),   λ = ln 2 / h,

where `h` is the **half-life** (weight 0.5 at age `h`; `h = ∞` ⇒ no
forgetting). The weight is converted into a per-observation noise variance

    σ_i² = σ_base² / max(w_i, 10⁻³),

so the GP is fitted with observation covariance `K + diag(σ_i²)`. Recent
observations get *low* effective noise (trusted), old ones *high*
effective noise (down-weighted but retained). This is **exact GP
regression** with heteroscedastic noise — no approximation. The target is
standardised with weighted mean/variance for consistency.

**ABO** = weighted GP + UCB (fixed β_base = 2), no change detection.

### 4.3 Drift detection — Page–Hinkley on standardized residuals

After evaluating `x_t`, we compute the standardized predictive residual of
the *pre-evaluation* posterior. Because `y_t` is noisy, its scale includes
both latent-function uncertainty and the configured observation noise:

    r_t = |y_t − μ_t(x_t)| /
          (sqrt(σ_f,t(x_t)^2 + σ_noise^2) + 10⁻¹²)

Under a well-calibrated model `r_t ≈ |N(0,1)|` (mean ≈ 0.8); when the
environment moves and the surrogate lags, `r_t` stays elevated. The
**Page–Hinkley** statistic accumulates deviations from the running mean
(since the last reset):

    G_t = max(0, G_{t−1} + (r_t − M_t − δ)),   M_t = mean of r since last reset

Drift is declared when `G_t > T_th`; on detection, `G` and the mean
accumulator reset (re-baselining on post-change residuals), so one change
produces one detection. We use `δ = 0.5`, `T_th = 25`, with a 10-sample
warm-up after each reset.

*Design note.* A **windowed** running mean would adapt to a *sustained*
level shift (pre-change data falls out of the window) and could then never
fire on it — we verified this failure mode while developing the detector.
The global (post-reset) mean lags the shift, which is what makes the
statistic grow.

### 4.4 DA-BO — drift-aware adaptive BO

At each iteration `t`:

1. compute weights `w_i` with the current half-life;
2. fit the weighted GP;
3. exploration level: stable → `β_t = β_base = 2`; within the recovery
   window after a detection → `β_t = β_high = 6`;
4. maximise `UCB_t(x) = μ_t(x) + β_t σ_t(x)` → `x_t`;
5. evaluate `y_t = f_t(x_t) + ε_t`;
6. compute `r_t`, update the Page–Hinkley detector;
7. **on detection**, for the next `L = 20` iterations: exploration boost
   (`β_high`), half-life halved (`h → h/2`), lengthscale halved
   (`ℓ → ℓ/2`) — *exploration*, *temporary increased forgetting*, and
   *shortened effective memory* respectively.

The current target value is **never** used to choose `x_t` before
evaluation (no lookahead; unit-tested by perturbation).

### 4.5 Fair comparison protocol

Within one (study, seed): all methods share (i) the same objective state
path, (ii) the same initial design (Latin hypercube from a dedicated
random stream), and (iii) the *same noise draw at every iteration* — each
method gets its own environment whose RNG is seeded identically, so draw
`t` of every method's stream is the same Gaussian (unit-tested). Budget
(100), domain, and seeds (20) are identical.

## 5. Experimental setup

### 5.1 Benchmarks

| Benchmark | Domain | Function | Optimum |
|---|---|---|---|
| `peak` (1D) | [0,1] | exp(−(x−μ_t)²/2s²), s=0.12 | x*_t = μ_t, f_opt = 1 |
| `peak2d` | [0,1]² | exp(−‖x−μ_t‖²/2s²), s=0.15 | x*_t = μ_t, f_opt = 1 |
| `multimodal` | [0,1] | Σ_j a_j(t) exp(−(x−m_j)²/w_j²), m = (0.2,0.5,0.8) | precomputed exactly (grid + golden section) |
| `sinusoidal` | [0,4π] | sin(x + φ_t) | two equivalent maxima, f_opt = 1 |
| `process` (3D) | T∈[150,250], P∈[1,5], S∈[20,80] | 10 + 82 Π Gaussian bumps | x*_t = (T_opt(t), P_opt(t), 45), f_opt = 92 |

All optima are computed **from the noise-free function** and precomputed
once per (study, seed) — identical for every method.

### 5.2 Non-stationarity scenarios

| Scenario | Definition |
|---|---|
| A stationary | f_t = f |
| B slow drift | μ_t = μ_0 + v t (v = 0.006/iter in 1D, 0.25→0.65 over the run; 0.004 in 2D) |
| C abrupt | μ jumps 0.25 → 0.75 (1D) / diagonal flip (2D) at t = 40 |
| D random walk | μ_{t+1} = clip(μ_t + η_t), η ~ N(0, σ_drift²), σ_drift = 0.008 |
| E noise shift | fixed f, σ_noise 0.02 → 0.15 at t = 50 |
| F amplitude switch | dominant peak 1 → 2 → 3 (t = 35, 70), smooth 4-iteration transitions |

### 5.3 Noise levels

σ ∈ {0.02 (low), 0.05 (medium), 0.15 (high), 0.30 (very high)} relative to
an objective of scale 1.

### 5.4 Budgets and seeds

Budget T = 100 evaluations (50/150 in the budget sensitivity study),
initial design n₀ = 10 (1D) / 16 (2D) / 12 (3D application, T = 80),
**20 seeds** for the main studies (10 for ablations, 5 for single-parameter
sweeps). Base seed 20260923; all streams derived deterministically from
`(study, seed, component)` via SHA-256 → PCG64.

### 5.5 Methods

`random_search`, `static_ei`, `static_ucb`, `static_pi`, `exploitation`,
`abo` (time-decayed, h = 32), `dabo` (DA-BO: h = 32, β_base = 2, β_high = 6,
L = 20, PH δ = 0.5, T_th = 25). The DA-BO configuration was fixed before
the final evaluation runs from problem-scale reasoning (half-life ≈ 1/3 of
the horizon; recovery ≈ 1/5; PH threshold ≈ 30 typical well-calibrated
residual standard deviations); §8 reports its neighbourhood rather than a
tuned optimum.

### 5.6 Computational setting

CPU-only (2 cores), Python 3.13, numpy/scipy/matplotlib. The full pipeline
takes ~35 minutes on two cores; every run is reproducible from
`python run_all.py`.

### 5.7 Statistical protocol

Multi-seed means ± std, 95% t-based CIs; **paired** bootstrap CIs (2000
resamples, methods paired by seed) and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for the
key method pairs; Holm–Bonferroni correction applied across the 19
pairwise tests reported in §6.

## 6. Results

### 6.1 RQ1 — Stationary objectives (Table 2)

On stationary 1D peaks, all GP methods beat random search (70.2) by ~8–10×:
static UCB 7.1–8.1, exploitation 7.0–7.8, ABO 7.1–10.7, EI 9.9–25.9,
PI 9.7–25.5, DA-BO 8.9–17.1 (low→high noise). Two observations:

* **PI and EI degrade sharply with noise** (PI: 9.7 → 25.5 from low to
  high noise). Both reference `f_best`, which is a *noisy* maximum; as
  noise grows, the reference becomes unreliable and the acquisition
  over-exploits around it. UCB has no such reference.
* **On the multimodal stationary landscape, all methods have wide
  variance** (UCB 10.5–16.6 ± 10–12): with only 100 evaluations, several
  seeds converge to the wrong (non-dominant) peak. This is a local-optima
  phenomenon, not a methodological artifact — and it motivates why a
  stationary GP *with* strong exploitation can look good or bad by seed.

**RQ2 (noise effect on acquisitions):** UCB is the most robust to
observation noise across all regimes (Tables 2 and 6); PI is the most
fragile. This is a direct consequence of the `f_best` reference in EI/PI.

### 6.2 RQ3 — Abrupt change (Table 4, Fig. 7)

The 1D abrupt jump (μ: 0.25 → 0.75 at t = 40) is the cleanest failure case
for stationary BO:

| method | CR | recovery time (to within 5%) | adaptation regret (30 it.) |
|---|---|---|---|
| random search | 70.4 ± 3.4 | 11.2 | 21.4 |
| static EI | 20.1 ± 3.1 | 16.4 | 10.1 |
| static UCB | 67.1 ± 0.3 | **never** | 30.0 |
| ABO (h=32) | 62.8 ± 4.1 | **never** | 29.9 |
| **DA-BO** | **16.4 ± 1.4** | **9.9** | **8.6** |

* Static UCB (and ABO) **never recover** to within 5% of the new optimum
  in any of the 20 seeds: after the jump, the GP is *confidently wrong* —
  low σ and stale high μ around the old peak, so UCB keeps exploiting it.
  ABO's forgetting alone (half-life 32) is too slow to erase ~40 confident
  observations within the remaining 60 iterations.
* **Static EI is surprisingly resilient (20.1)**: after the jump, `f_best`
  becomes unattainable, so EI(x) ≈ (μ − f_best)Φ(Z) + σφ(Z) behaves like a
  pessimistic UCB and the optimizer eventually wanders to the new peak.
  This is an accidental, uncontrolled form of "exploration after
  failure" — it works, but with 16.4-iteration recovery it is slower than
  DA-BO's detection-driven response, and it does not generalize (see the
  2D row).
* **DA-BO detects the jump at the first post-change evaluation in all 20
  seeds (mean lag 0.0)** — the first mismatching residual is large
  (r ≈ |0 − 1|/σ ≫ threshold) — and recovers within ~10 iterations
  (Fig. 7). CR is reduced by **75.7%** (67.12 → 16.37; paired bootstrap CI
  for the difference: −51.37 to −50.08; Wilcoxon p < 10⁻⁵).

2D abrupt: static UCB 50.0 ± 10.9, ABO 45.6, static EI 63.6, DA-BO
53.2 ± 8.5. All methods struggle (the 2D posterior is flatter and the jump
is less visible in one residual), and DA-BO is *not* significantly better
than static UCB (CI −0.56 to +6.74, p = 0.09). See §9 for the analysis.

### 6.3 RQ4/RQ5 — Slow drift (Table 3, Figs. 3–6)

1D slow drift (μ: 0.15 → 0.75 over 100 iterations):

| method | CR | tracking error |
|---|---|---|
| random search | 69.7 ± 3.6 | 0.28 |
| static UCB | 74.2 ± 2.5 | 0.29 |
| ABO (h=32) | 66.8 ± 3.5 | 0.24 |
| **DA-BO** | **26.7 ± 2.0** | **0.11** |

Static UCB does *worse than random search* (74.2 vs 69.7): the GP's mean
lags the peak by a roughly constant offset, UCB keeps querying just behind
the moving peak, and the lag error accumulates every iteration. ABO halves
the lag (66.8) but a fixed half-life still cannot keep up with a *continuous*
change. DA-BO's detector fires repeatedly as the lag re-accumulates (72
events across 20 seeds; each event resets memory and boosts exploration),
holding the tracking error at 0.11 and cutting CR by **64%**
(bootstrap CI −48.4 to −46.6, p < 10⁻⁵).

2D slow drift inverts the picture: static UCB 46.6 ± 8.5, ABO 46.1,
DA-BO **54.7 ± 6.1 (worse; CI +5.1 to +11.2, p = 10⁻⁴)**. Analysis (§9):
in 2D the residual signal of a slow diagonal drift is weak, so the
detector's events are largely noise-triggered, and each event wastes
iterations on boosted exploration away from the (correct) tracking
trajectory.

Multimodal amplitude hand-over (peak 1 → 2 → 3): static UCB 63.7 ± 9.0,
ABO 67.5, **DA-BO 24.8 ± 6.7** — a **61% reduction** (CI −43.7 to −35.0,
p < 10⁻⁵). Abandoning a *confidently* tracked dominant peak is exactly
where the detection + boost combination pays off.

Random walk (σ = 0.008): static UCB 13.0 ± 6.1, ABO 11.9, DA-BO 15.5 ±
2.7 (slightly worse; CI +0.00 to +4.60, p = 0.008). At this slow rate,
static tracking is adequate and the detector's false alarms cost more than
they save.

### 6.4 RQ7 — Robustness

* **Noise level under abrupt change** (Table 6): DA-BO stays in
  16.3–23.5 across σ = 0.02–0.30 vs static UCB ~65–67; the *cost* of
  adaptation grows with noise (false alarms get cheaper to trigger), but
  the *benefit* is preserved 4× over the noise range.
* **Pure noise shift** (function fixed, σ 0.02 → 0.15 at t=50): static
  UCB 7.35, ABO 7.52, DA-BO **11.47 (+56%; CI +3.7 to +4.7, p < 10⁻⁵)**.
  The detector reacts to the residual level shift (74 events in 20 seeds)
  and the responses buy nothing because the landscape didn't move — the
  cleanest illustration that *residual inflation is a signal about model
  mismatch, not about the objective moving*.
* **Kernel** (Table 8): DA-BO 26.0 (Matérn 5/2) / 24.5 (Matérn 3/2) /
  25.6 (RBF) on slow 1D drift — results are kernel-insensitive within
  this family.
* **Budget**: at T = 50/100/150, 1D abrupt CR is 17.6/16.3/15.1 for DA-BO
  vs 36.9/67.0/97.1 for static UCB — the gap *widens* with budget, because
  static BO's post-change regret keeps accumulating.
* **Initial design size**: smaller n₀ (6) helps DA-BO (23.9 vs 32.2 at
  n₀=16) — less stale confident data to forget.

### 6.5 RQ5 — Exploration vs exploitation (Figs. 3, 10)

Fig. 3 (trajectories) and Fig. 10 (acquisition at t = change+2) show the
mechanism: after the jump, static UCB's acquisition is still peaked at the
stale optimum (μ ≈ 1 there, low σ), while DA-BO — in its recovery window
with β = 6 and a halved lengthscale — puts substantial mass on the
unexplored side of the domain and finds the new peak within ~3 iterations.
Fig. 5 shows DA-BO's rolling instantaneous regret returning to the pre-change
level within ~10 iterations, while static methods' regret floor rises
permanently.

### 6.6 Real-world-inspired application (Table 9)

3D process efficiency (temperature/pressure/speed; T_opt drifts 190 →
202 °C over 80 iterations — equipment degradation; P_opt oscillates with a
60-iteration demand cycle; noise σ = 1.0 on a [10, 92] scale):

| method | CR | final efficiency | tracking error |
|---|---|---|---|
| random search | 5361 ± 118 | 26.6 ± 15.9 | 31.7 ± 1.2 |
| static EI | 4130 ± 1387 | 45.3 ± 31.6 | 24.6 ± 8.5 |
| static UCB | 4120 ± 1375 | 35.5 ± 30.0 | 23.0 ± 8.9 |
| ABO | 4341 ± 1313 | 28.7 ± 19.5 | 25.9 ± 8.9 |
| DA-BO | 4341 ± 1313 | 28.7 ± 19.5 | 25.9 ± 8.9 |

All GP methods beat random search by ~23%. Two findings:

1. **DA-BO never triggers** (0 drift events in all 10 seeds): the optimum
   moves only ~12 °C over the run — a small fraction of the temperature
   bump width (30 °C) — while P_opt's oscillation (±0.8 bar) is barely
   larger than its bump width (1.8 bar). The per-iteration model mismatch
   is small relative to noise, so the residual stream never trips the
   detector; DA-BO reduces to ABO (identical trajectories).
2. **The 3D run is dominated by a different problem**: the pressure ridge
   is narrow (width 1.8 on a range of 4), and several seeds' 12-point LHS
   plus UCB exploration miss or partially miss it, producing the large
   variance (±1300). Here *initial coverage* matters more than
   adaptivity — a limitation to address with space-filling initial designs
   or multi-fidelity evaluation (noted in §12).

This is an honest applicability boundary: DA-BO's benefit scales with the
*speed of the optimum relative to the noise scale and the GP width*, not
with the mere presence of drift.

## 7. Ablations (Table 7, Fig. 11)

**Components (1D abrupt, 10 seeds; Δ vs full DA-BO, CR = 17.7 ± 2.8):**

| removed component | CR | Δ |
|---|---|---|
| nothing (full) | 17.7 ± 2.8 | — |
| drift detection | 60.6 ± 5.2 | **+243%** |
| memory shortening (h/2 and ℓ/2 during recovery) | 50.3 ± 14.0 | +185% |
| exploration boost (β 2 → 6) | 35.3 ± 10.0 | +100% |
| forgetting (h = ∞) | 33.3 ± 10.7 | +89% |

* **Detection is the dominant component** — without it the optimizer is
  ABO, which cannot escape a confidently-wrong GP (§6.2).
* The recovery responses all matter individually (boost, memory
  shortening, forgetting); together they convert a detection into a fast
  re-localisation.
* On 2D abrupt the picture inverts: removing detection or the boost makes
  things *better* (45.8/45.2 vs full 54.7), confirming §9's false-alarm
  diagnosis in the weak-signal 2D regime.

**Parameter sweeps (1D abrupt, 10 seeds):**

* **Half-life** (RQ6): h = 8 → 19.7, 16 → 16.6, 32 → 17.7, 64 → 24.3,
  128 → 28.6, ∞ → 33.3. Optimal h ≈ 1/6 of the horizon; too short
  (h = 8) increases variance of the surrogate; too long and the old peak
  dominates. The frozen value h = 32 is within 6% of the best.
* **PH threshold**: 10 → 18.8, 25 → 17.7, 50 → 17.6, 100 → 17.1 — a flat
  plateau; detection timing is not threshold-sensitive here because the
  abrupt residual spike is large.
* **Recovery length**: 10 → 18.1, 20 → 17.7, 40 → 18.6 — also flat within
  ±6%; longer windows waste some post-recovery budget on boosted
  exploration.

## 8. Sensitivity analysis (Table 8)

* **Exploration β (slow 1D drift):** static UCB improves monotonically
  73.4 → 57.2 as β goes 1 → 8 (more exploration helps tracking); DA-BO is
  flat at ~26 across all β — its performance is governed by the detection
  logic, not the base β.
* **Budget (abrupt):** see §6.4 — DA-BO's advantage grows with budget in
  1D; in 2D the two methods are statistically indistinguishable at all
  budgets.
* **Initial design size (slow 1D):** n₀ = 6 → DA-BO 23.9, n₀ = 16 → 32.2;
  smaller initial designs leave less confident stale data. Static UCB is
  flat (73.5/72.4/76.2).
* **Noise (abrupt 1D):** static UCB flat ~65–67; DA-BO 21.2 (low) →
  16.3 (medium) → 17.7 (high) → 23.5 (very high). The benefit persists
  over a 15× noise range.
* **Drift magnitude (random walk σ):** static UCB 10.4 (σ=0.004) → 19.0
  (0.008) → 34.2 (0.016); DA-BO 15.1 → 17.2 → 20.0. As drift strengthens,
  static degrades 3.3× while DA-BO degrades 1.3× — adaptation insurance
  pays as the environment becomes more variable (at the smallest drift,
  DA-BO's false-alarm cost makes it slightly worse: 15.1 vs 10.4).
* **Kernel:** §6.4 — insensitive within {Matérn 5/2, Matérn 3/2, RBF}.

## 9. Failure analysis — when adaptive BO loses (RQ8)

1. **Stationary objectives (RQ8a).** DA-BO costs +26% (low noise),
   +48% (medium), +110% (high) vs static UCB on the 1D peak (Table 2).
   Two mechanisms: (i) forgetting discards useful data — on a fixed
   objective, older observations are *not* stale; (ii) false alarms — in
   the medium-noise stationary study, 9/20 seeds experience at least one
   false detection (17 events total), each wasting up to 20 iterations of
   boosted exploration. The cost scales with noise because false alarms
   scale with the residual variance.
2. **Pure noise shifts (RQ8b).** +56% vs static (Table 6): the detector
   correctly flags that *the model no longer matches the data*, but the
   prescribed response (explore + forget) addresses *objective* change,
   not *noise* change. A noise-adaptive version (e.g., re-estimating σ
   before exploring) is future work.
3. **2D slow drift (RQ8c).** DA-BO is significantly *worse* (+17%,
   p = 10⁻⁴). In 2D the per-iteration mismatch from a slow diagonal drift
   produces small, noisy residuals; the detector fires on residual
   *fluctuations* rather than the drift itself, and each false event
   steers the optimizer away from the tracking trajectory it would
   otherwise follow. The same inversion appears in the 2D ablations
   (removing detection improves CR). The lesson: **the detector's signal
   strength depends on dimension and drift geometry**, and the response
   should be gated on signal quality.
4. **Slow random walk (RQ8d).** +20% vs static: below the drift rate where
   the GP's own uncertainty naturally tracks the optimum, adaptation is
   pure overhead.
5. **3D application (RQ8e).** No detections at all (§6.6): when the
   optimum's motion is small relative to noise and kernel width, the
   residual signal is sub-threshold and DA-BO degenerates to ABO — which
   itself trails static UCB there because the 3D problem is dominated by
   initial-coverage variance, not by staleness.

In no case does DA-BO catastrophically fail: its worst outcomes are
moderate overheads (20–110%) relative to a strong baseline, never worse
than random search.

## 10. Limitations

* **Dimensionality.** The grid-based acquisition optimizer and isotropic
  kernels limit direct transfer to d ≳ 5; the 2D results already show the
  detector's signal weakening.
* **Synthetic optima.** Regret/tracking require known noise-free optima;
  the 3D application is a *controlled simulator inspired by* process
  optimization, not a plant.
* **Known noise level.** The GP's assumed σ_base is set from the
  experiment configuration; DA-BO does not *estimate* the observation
  noise (which is why pure noise shifts confuse it).
* **No theoretical guarantees.** No regret bounds are established; all
  claims are empirical.
* **Fixed detector parameters.** δ = 0.5, T_th = 25 across all studies;
  adaptive thresholding (calibrated false-alarm rate) is future work.
* **Seed budget.** 20 seeds per main configuration is solid but not
  exhaustive; several 2D cells carry ±10–12 CR uncertainty (10 seeds for
  ablations).
* **One response policy.** DA-BO uses a single fixed recovery recipe
  (β_high = 3 β_base, h/2, ℓ/2, L = 20); richer response policies
  (e.g., partial history reset, kernel re-estimation) are unexplored.

---

## 11. Conclusion

We asked how Bayesian optimization should adapt when the objective is noisy
and changes, and answered it empirically with controlled benchmarks,
ablations, and a 3D application. The evidence:

1. **Stationary regime:** standard GP+UCB (and pure exploitation) are
   near-optimal among the methods tested; adaptive forgetting only costs.
   Acquisition choice matters more than people assume under noise: UCB is
   the most robust, PI the most fragile.
2. **Changing 1D regime:** drift-aware adaptation (detection + recovery
   response + forgetting) reduces cumulative regret by 61–76% across
   abrupt change, slow drift, and amplitude hand-over, with fast measured
   recovery (~10 iterations after an abrupt jump) — and the gains are
   ablation-attributable, dominated by *detection*.
3. **Boundary of the benefit:** the benefit scales with the ratio of
   optimum-motion speed to (noise + kernel width). Below that ratio —
   2D slow drift, slow random walk, pure noise shift, and the 3D
   application — adaptation is neutral to harmful. A stationary objective
   is the limiting case where it is strictly harmful.
4. **Forgetting alone is not enough:** time-decayed BO (ABO) reduces the
   tracking lag under continuous drift but cannot escape a
   *confidently wrong* GP after an abrupt change; the detector is what
   converts stale certainty into actionable uncertainty.

The practical prescription that falls out: use standard BO when the
objective is stationary or slowly varying relative to its own noise;
use a drift-aware adaptive strategy when the expected rate of objective
change is comparable to or larger than the per-iteration model mismatch —
and monitor the adaptation's own cost (false alarms), which grows with
noise.

## 12. Future work

* **Contextual BO.** Observe a context `c_t` (temperature, demand regime,
  operating condition) and model `f(x, c)` with `z = [x, c]` as GP input,
  choosing `x*_t = argmax_x f(x, c_t)`. The environment supplies `c_t`;
  the optimizer does not choose it. This reframes "non-stationarity
  from known environmental variables" as a smoother, learnable problem —
  and distinguishes cleanly from contextual *bandits* (repeated cheap
  decisions with observed context) because the action space here is
  expensive and continuous and the surrogate's structure does the work.
* **Noise-adaptive response.** Estimate `σ_noise(t)` online (e.g., from
  residual calibration) and gate the exploration response on *objective*
  vs *noise* mismatch — directly addressing failure modes RQ8a/b.
* **Signal-qualified detection.** Dimension-aware or geometry-aware
  thresholds (e.g., requiring sustained multi-directional residual
  inflation) to fix the 2D false-alarm problem (RQ8c).
* **Multi-fidelity and constrained BO under drift** for the narrow-ridge
  3D application (initial-coverage problem, §6.6).
* **Theory.** Regret bounds for time-decayed GP under bounded path
  variation of `x*_t`; calibration of the PH threshold to a target
  false-alarm rate.

## Appendix A — Exact formulas

**Weighted GP.** History `{(x_i, y_i, t_i)}`, current time `t`, half-life
`h`:

    w_i    = clip( exp(−(ln 2 / h)(t − t_i)), 10⁻³, 1 )
    σ_i²   = σ_base² / w_i
    ȳ, s²  = weighted mean / variance of {y_i} with weights {w_i}
    y_i'   = (y_i − ȳ)/s,   V = K(x, x) + diag(σ_i²/s²)
    α      = V⁻¹ y'
    μ(x)   = ȳ + s · k(x, X)ᵀ α
    σ(x)   = s · sqrt( k(x,x) − k(x, X)ᵀ V¹ k(x, X) )

**Page–Hinkley.** Predictive residual
`r_t = |y_t − μ_t(x_t)|/(sqrt(σ_f,t(x_t)^2 + σ_noise^2) + 10⁻¹²)`:

    M_t = (Σ_{i≤t} r_i) / t      (since last reset)
    G_t = max( 0, G_{t−1} + r_t − M_t − δ )
    detect ⇔ G_t > T_th  →  reset {G, Σ, t} to zero;  warm-up: 10 samples

**DA-BO recovery window** (after a detection at time `t_d`, for
`t ∈ (t_d, t_d + L]`, L = 20):

    β_t = β_high (6)      h_t = h / 2      ℓ_t = ℓ / 2
